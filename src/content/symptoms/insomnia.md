## What It Is & Why It Happens

**Insomnia** during pregnancy is characterized by difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or experiencing non-restorative sleep, despite having adequate opportunity to sleep. It affects up to 80% of pregnant individuals.

During the first trimester, hormonal shifts (specifically rising progesterone) can disrupt your circadian rhythm, leading to daytime fatigue and nighttime wakefulness. Physical discomforts, such as morning sickness, breast tenderness, and early-stage back pain, also prevent restful sleep.

In the third trimester, the primary obstacles to sleep are physical. The large abdomen makes it difficult to find a comfortable position. Additionally, the baby’s active movements, pressure on the bladder (requiring multiple trips to the bathroom), heartburn, and leg cramps frequently wake you throughout the night. Emotional stress and anxiety regarding labor, delivery, and parenting also stimulate the nervous system, preventing sleep.

## Typical Timeline

Insomnia can occur at any stage, but its triggers change:
*   **First Trimester (Weeks 4-12):** Common. Driven by hormonal surges, nausea, and frequent urination.
*   **Second Trimester (Weeks 13-27):** Often improves as early symptoms subside and physical size is still manageable.
*   **Third Trimester (Weeks 28-40):** Peak severity. Driven by physical size, joint pain, heartburn, baby movements, and labor anxiety.

## Safe Comfort Measures

You can improve sleep quality safely with these behavioral sleep hygiene practices:
*   **Establish a Bedtime Routine:** Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day to reinforce your body's natural sleep-wake cycle. Keep your bedroom cool, dark, and quiet.
*   **Optimize Sleep Position:** Sleep on your left side to maximize blood flow to the uterus and kidneys. Use a body pillow or place pillows between your knees, under your abdomen, and behind your back for support.
*   **Practice Sleep Hygiene:** Avoid screens (phones, TVs, tablets) for at least 1 hour before bed. The blue light from screens suppresses melatonin production.
*   **Manage Diet and Fluids:** Drink plenty of water during the day, but limit fluids in the evening to reduce nighttime bathroom trips. Avoid heavy, spicy, or acidic meals close to bedtime to prevent heartburn.
*   **Engage in Wind-down Activities:** Practice gentle prenatal yoga, deep breathing exercises, or read a book before bed. Avoid strenuous exercise within 3 hours of bedtime.

## When to Call the Doctor (Red Flags)

Occasional insomnia is standard, but severe sleep deprivation or symptoms of underlying medical conditions (like sleep apnea or depression) require clinical evaluation.

**Contact your healthcare provider if you experience:**
*   Persistent, severe insomnia that interferes with your ability to function during the day.
*   **Signs of Sleep Apnea:** Loud, chronic snoring accompanied by gasping or choking sounds during sleep, or extreme daytime sleepiness.
*   **Restless Legs Syndrome:** An irresistible urge to move your legs, accompanied by crawling or tingling sensations that worsen in the evening or at night.
*   Insomnia accompanied by persistent sadness, hopelessness, lack of energy, or thoughts of self-harm (signs of prenatal depression).
